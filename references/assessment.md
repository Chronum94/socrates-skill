# Assessment

How to tell whether understanding is real, and how to close a session honestly.

## Why "yes, I understand" is worthless

Self-reported understanding is uncorrelated with actual understanding, and the
error runs in both directions: fluent material feels understood when it isn't
(the fluency illusion), and hard-won understanding often feels shakier than it
is. A learner who has just been walked to a conclusion is at peak confidence and
minimum evidence.

Never accept assent as evidence. Never ask "does that make sense?" — it is a
question whose only real answer is yes.

## What counts as evidence

Roughly ordered by strength:

| Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|
| Solves a novel case correctly, unaided | Strong |
| Predicts an outcome correctly *before* seeing it | Strong |
| Explains it to a naive audience without jargon | Strong |
| Identifies where their own reasoning could break | Strong |
| States the general principle in their own words | Moderate |
| Corrects a plausible wrong version | Moderate |
| Recognizes the right answer among options | Weak |
| Repeats the explanation back | Very weak |
| Says they understand | None |

## The transfer test

The default close. Pose a case the learner has not seen that requires the same
principle, and let them work it alone.

**Designing one.** Change the surface, hold the structure fixed. Good axes to
vary:

- **Surface features** — different names, units, setting, domain
- **Direction** — give them the output, ask for the input
- **A boundary case** — empty, zero, one, infinite, degenerate
- **A near-miss** — a case that looks like it needs the principle but doesn't,
  and ask which it is
- **The negative** — "make it fail on purpose"
- **A whole different domain** — the strongest test of all, if the principle is
  general enough to survive it

**What a good transfer test is not:** the same problem with the numbers changed.
That tests execution, which they already demonstrated.

**Calibrate the difficulty.** A transfer test they fail is informative but
demoralizing as a session ender. Aim for one they can get with effort, and if
they can't, treat it as data — the understanding wasn't there yet — and go back
in without making it a verdict.

## Teach-back

Better than transfer for conceptual and interpretive material, where there's no
crisp novel case to pose.

> Explain this to me as if I'm a smart person who's never seen it. No jargon.

The gaps show up as: jargon standing in for explanation, hand-waving at exactly
the load-bearing step, inability to say *why* rather than *what*, and skipping
the part they don't understand. Probe those specifically.

A fluent talker can bluff teach-back, which is why it pairs well with a follow-up
transfer case for anything procedural.

## Choosing between them

- **Procedural, derivable, computational** → transfer test
- **Conceptual, interpretive, argumentative** → teach-back
- **They're about to act on it** → both, and check the failure modes explicitly
- **Short session, low stakes** → one good prediction question is enough

## Diagnosing what a wrong answer reveals

A wrong answer is the most information-dense thing in the session. Read it.

| Pattern | Likely cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Right procedure, wrong step | Slip, not misunderstanding | Point at the step; don't re-teach |
| Consistent systematic error | A rule they learned wrong | Find and repair the rule itself |
| Right in the trained case, wrong when varied | Pattern-matching, not understanding | Back to the principle |
| Answer unrelated to the question | Missing premise, or asked past them | Diagnose which, then supply or decompose |
| Correct but can't justify | Recall or luck | Vary until recall stops helping |
| Confident and wrong | An intact, wrong model | Elicit → predict → confront (`pedagogy.md`) |
| Hedged and right | Low confidence, real knowledge | Say so — calibration is worth correcting too |

Calibration deserves attention in both directions. A learner who is right but
doesn't trust it will not act on what they know, and telling them their
confidence is too low is useful feedback.

## Closing a session

Keep it short. Four beats:

1. **Transfer test or teach-back**, per above.
2. **The principle, in their words.** Not a summary of the conversation — a
   statement of what is now true. "So what's the rule?"
3. **What's still open.** Name honestly anything unresolved, including anything
   you flagged as uncertain. Do not paper over an unfinished thread to end on a
   clean note.
4. **One thread to pull alone.** A single specific question they could go
   investigate, not a reading list.

Congratulate briefly and specifically or not at all. "You got the invariant on
your own, which is the hard part" is worth something; "great job!" is noise.

## When to stop without success

Not every session lands, and pushing through fatigue produces nothing worth
keeping. Stop cleanly when answers are degrading, when the learner is
frustrated, when time is up, or when you've established that a prerequisite is
missing.

Say what happened without dressing it up, and leave a foothold for next time.

> I think the thing in the way is the chain rule — that's what we kept running
> into. Worth an hour on that first; this'll be much easier afterward.

That is a successful diagnosis, not a failed session.
